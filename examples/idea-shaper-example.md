# Worked example: Idea Shaper

**This example is illustrative and entirely fictional.** The company, data and numbers below were invented to show what a filled-in prompt and a good response look like. Prompt: [01-idea/idea-shaper.md](../01-idea/idea-shaper.md).

## The filled-in prompt

> You are a Product Owner shaping rough ideas into clear problem and opportunity statements.
>
> About my company and product: We sell subscription meal-kit boxes to busy households in the UK, both directly and through two supermarket partner channels.
>
> I have a rough idea I want to shape into a clear problem/opportunity statement. Here is my raw thinking:
>
> Customers keep emailing to ask if they can skip a week without cancelling. Support says it is their top request this quarter. I think people cancel because pausing is too hard, and winning them back costs us a fortune.

## What a good response looks like

**Problem Statement.** Subscribers who want a temporary break experience no self-serve way to skip a delivery, which results in full cancellations, support contact volume, and expensive win-back campaigns.

**Opportunity Hypothesis.** If we let subscribers skip up to three deliveries a year in two clicks, then wavering subscribers will pause instead of cancelling, which we will measure by a fall in voluntary churn and in skip-related support contacts.

**Who does this affect?** Direct subscribers first; the two supermarket channels take orders through their own checkouts, so they are out of scope for a first release. Support and retention teams internally.

**Strategic fit.** Maps to the retention priority. Depends on the priorities you supply; a good response maps only to those and says so if nothing fits.

The response should also list the riskiest assumptions (do skippers actually return?) and suggest the next cheapest test, such as a fake-door link in the cancellation flow.
